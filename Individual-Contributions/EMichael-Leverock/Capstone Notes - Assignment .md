**EU AI Act**



Below is a concise summary of **Articles 9, 10, 13, Annex III, and 17 of the European Union AI Act (Regulation (EU) 2024/1689)** based on the structure and content outlined in the AI Act Explorer.



Article 9 – Risk Management System



Purpose: Obligates providers of high-risk AI systems to establish and maintain a comprehensive risk management system.



Key Points:



Identify, assess, and mitigate risks throughout the lifecycle of a high-risk AI system.



Continuously monitor and update risk assessments when new information arises.



Document all risk management activities and ensure they are auditable.



Mitigation measures must reduce risks to the lowest possible level without impeding the system’s functionality.



Scope: Applies to all high-risk AI systems listed in Annex III.



Article 10 – Data and Data Governance



Purpose: Establishes requirements for the quality, governance, and management of datasets used by high-risk AI systems.



Key Points:



Data used must be relevant, representative, free of errors, and appropriate for the intended purpose.

Implement measures to prevent bias, errors, or unfair outcomes.

Maintain thorough documentation of data sources, preprocessing steps, and evaluation.

Ensure ongoing monitoring of datasets for updates or corrections.



Governance Measures: Include access control, data consistency checks, and verification procedures to maintain integrity.





Article 13 – Transparency and Provision of Information to Deployers



Purpose: Imposes a transparency obligation on developers/providers to furnish deployers with key information.

Key Points:



Provide detailed instructions and information regarding intended purpose, operational context, risks, and limitations.

Information must allow deployers to use the AI system safely and reliably.

Include documentation about system performance, known biases, and recommended mitigation strategies.



Goal: Ensure deployers understand operational risks to protect end-users and affected parties.





Annex III – High-Risk AI Systems



Purpose: Lists autonomous AI systems classified explicitly as high-risk within the EU framework.

Categories Include:



Biometric identification and categorization systems: Facial recognition, iris scanning, fingerprint technologies.

Critical infrastructure: Systems controlling energy, transport, and essential utilities.

Education and vocational training: AI recommending grades, exam scoring.

Employment and worker management: AI recruitment, selection, performance evaluation tools.

Law enforcement: AI tools for predictive policing, crime analytics, social scoring.

Migration, asylum, and border control: Risk-assessment of asylum applications or border checks.

Education, public services, and safety: AI systems impacting individual rights or legal status.



Significance: Classification in Annex III triggers strict obligations under Articles 9, 10, and other risk management and compliance provisions.





Article 17 – Quality Management System



Purpose: Mandates the implementation of a quality management system (QMS) by providers of high-risk AI systems.



Key Points:



Establish internal procedures, responsibilities, and workflows to ensure conformity with the AI Act.

Integrate documentation, record-keeping, continuous improvement, and corrective measures into the QMS.

Ensure that human oversight, risk management, and data governance align with legal and regulatory requirements.

Objective: Guarantee consistent development and deployment of AI systems under a controlled, traceable process.





Synthesis



The combination of Articles 9, 10, 13, and 17, along with Annex III, creates a regulatory framework for high-risk AI systems focusing on:



Formal risk assessment and mitigation.

Rigorous data governance.

Transparency to deployers for safe operational use.

Compliance-driven quality management processes.

Clear definitions of high-risk categories triggering these obligations.



These provisions collectively aim to minimize risks to fundamental rights, safety, and fairness, ensuring high-risk AI systems are deployed responsibly in the EU internal market.





**ISO/IEC 42001:2023 - Clauses 6.1, 8.4, Annex A**



Based on publicly available ISO 42001 summaries and guidance resources, the requested sections can be summarized as follows:



Clause 6.1: Actions to Address Risks and Opportunities



Purpose: Ensures organizations identify, assess, and manage AI-related risks and opportunities to operate a compliant and effective AI Management System (AIMS).



Sub-clauses:



6.1.1 General



Identify AI-related risks and opportunities that could influence the intended outcomes of the AIMS.

Take actions to prevent problems and improve the system continuously.



Maintain documented evidence of actions taken.



6.1.2 AI Risk Assessment



Implement a consistent process to evaluate AI system risks.

Assess likelihood, severity, and impact of potential issues on people, society, and organizational objectives.

Keep records of all risk assessment results.



6.1.3 AI Risk Treatment



Develop and document a plan to manage identified AI risks.

Map mitigation measures to relevant controls in Annex A.

Include additional controls if necessary and secure management approval for high-significance risks.



6.1.4 AI System Impact Assessment



Evaluate potential impacts of AI systems on individuals, groups, and society, considering misuse and intended use.

Document findings to improve overall AI risk management.



Clause 8.4: AI System Impact Assessment



Purpose: Establishes a structured process for operational assessment of AI system impacts post-deployment.



Key Requirements:



Conduct regular impact assessments for AI systems within scope.



Document and retain findings of such assessments.



Focus on both organizational and societal impacts of AI, especially after major changes.



Ensure the assessment informs mitigation and improvement actions within the AIMS.





Annex A: Reference Control Objectives and Controls



Purpose: Provides 38 AI-specific controls, organized into nine sections, to address specific risks identified during planning and risk assessment.



Key Sections (Summary):



Policies Related to AI

AI policy approval, alignment with organizational policies, periodic review.



Internal Organization

Define AI roles, responsibilities, and reporting mechanisms for concerns.



Resources for AI Systems

Documentation and allocation of data, compute, tooling, system, and human resources.



Assessing Impacts of AI Systems

AI system impact assessment process, documentation, evaluation of individual and societal effects.



AI System Life Cycle

Objectives for responsible development, design processes, requirements, architecture documentation, verification, deployment, monitoring, technical documentation, and event logging.



Data for AI Systems

Data selection, acquisition, quality assurance, provenance, and preparation procedures.



Information for Interested Parties

User/system documentation, external reporting, incident communications, stakeholder information.



Use of AI Systems

Implementation of acceptable use processes, objectives for responsible AI use, intended use documentation.



Third-Party and Customer Relationships

Responsibilities allocation, supplier assessments, customer communication, and compliance obligations.



Conceptual Role of Annex A:



These controls translate the high-level AIMS requirements into operational safeguards.

Organizations select controls applicable to their context based on their risk assessment.

A Statement of Applicability (SoA) documents selected/excluded controls with justification.



References for Textual Structure



Clause 6.1 aligns with ISO 42001’s planning and risk management cycle.

Clause 8.4 operationalizes AI system impact monitoring.

Annex A provides the actionable control framework underpinning Clauses 6–10.

ISO/IEC 42001:2023 establishes comprehensive requirements for managing Artificial Intelligence (AI) systems responsibly, and Annex A Control A.6 specifically focuses on the AI system lifecycle, ensuring that organizations integrate ethical, technical, and operational considerations from conception to retirement. Below is a structured



overview:



1\. Life Cycle Stages



ISO 42001 defines several key stages in the AI system lifecycle:



Planning and Requirements Definition



Identify system goals, operational context, and intended outcomes.

Establish data requirements and ethical considerations (fairness, transparency, privacy).

Ensure compliance with legal and regulatory frameworks.



Design and Development



Create AI models, algorithms, and system architecture aligned with responsible AI principles.

Incorporate provisions for human oversight, testing, and impact assessments.

Document design specifications, expected limitations, and success criteria.



Testing, Verification, and Validation



Verify that the AI system meets design specifications and performs reliably.

Evaluate safety, fairness, accuracy, and societal impact.

Define criteria and metrics for performance and ethical standards.



Deployment and Operation



Implement the system in production securely and responsibly.

Monitor performance, detect unintended behaviors, and maintain incident management mechanisms.

Ensure operational controls and adherence to risk mitigation strategies.



Monitoring and Review



Continuously assess AI system performance against established objectives.

Evaluate compliance, detect model drift, and apply corrective actions.

Integrate feedback loops for ongoing improvement and risk management.



Continuous Improvement



Update policies, processes, and controls based on audits and performance data.

Adapt to technological evolution, changing requirements, and new risks.

Apply lessons learned to refine the lifecycle management framework.



Decommissioning and Retirement



Safely phase out obsolete AI systems.

Secure residual data, mitigate risks, and ensure ethical handling of AI artifacts.





2\. Core Requirements and Best Practices



ISO 42001 mandates organizations to:



Document Life Cycle Processes: Maintain accessible, auditable records for each stage.



Integrate Ethical Objectives: Embed fairness, privacy, transparency, and societal impact in planning, development, and operation.



Require Human Oversight: Ensure human responsibility is maintained across decision-making and operational phases.



Establish Verification \& Validation Measures: Conduct rigorous testing to certify reliability, safety, and compliance.



Deploy Monitoring Mechanisms: Track system behavior continuously, including KPIs and risk indicators.



Enable Traceability \& Explainability: Maintain full decision logs and clear explanations of AI operations.



Conduct AI Impact Assessments: Evaluate and document potential consequences on individuals, groups, and broader society.



Implement Continuous Risk Management: Identify, treat, and monitor risks throughout the lifecycle, updating controls as needed.





3\. Tools and Operational Support



Though ISO 42001 does not prescribe specific technologies, it emphasizes:



Lifecycle Management Platforms: For structured tracking of development, deployment, and retirement activities.



Risk \& Impact Assessment Tools: To evaluate ethical, legal, technical, and societal risks.



Audit and Documentation Frameworks: Ensuring transparency, traceability, and regulatory compliance.





4\. Alignment with Governance Frameworks



Lifecycle management is integrated into the broader Artificial Intelligence Management System (AIMS) of the organization.



Uses PDCA (Plan–Do–Check–Act) for continuous improvement.



Supports compliance with other standards (ISO 31000 for risk, ISO 27001 for security) and regulatory frameworks (e.g., EU AI Act).



Final Summary



ISO 42001 requires that AI lifecycle management:



Covers full end-to-end stages from planning and development through monitoring and retirement.

Ensures ethical, transparent, accountable, and traceable AI operations.

Incorporates risk-based controls and ongoing performance evaluation.

Provides documented oversight and continuous improvement, enabling organizations to demonstrate responsible AI governance.



These requirements transform AI management into a structured, auditable, and ethically aligned system, shaping AI development and deployment to maximize trust, compliance, and operational reliability.





**NIST AI RMF**



Below is a structured summary of the specified subcategories from the **NIST AI Risk Management Framework** (AI RMF) based on the most current guidance.



1\. MAP 1.5 – Organizational Risk Tolerance Definition



Objective: Define and document how much AI-related risk the organization is willing to accept for each system.



Requirements:



Establish the maximum allowable risk thresholds for AI systems, above which deployment is restricted or the system requires decommissioning.



Document decisions on risk trade-offs, including considerations for safety, financial, operational, reputational, and societal impacts.



Allocate oversight and resources according to risk level; higher-risk systems receive more rigorous monitoring.



Ensure context-specific risks are reviewed, including off-label or emergent AI applications.



Record all decisions related to risk tolerance in a transparent, retrievable manner for internal audit and regulatory purposes.



Implementation Notes:



Tie risk tolerances to organizational values, mission, and externally mandated compliance requirements.

Integrate cross-functional review with stakeholder feedback where applicable.





2\. MAP 2.1 – AI System Characterization / Task Definition



Objective: Clearly define what AI tasks and methods the system will perform.



Requirements:



Enumerate specific tasks the AI system is intended to support (e.g., classification, recommendation, content generation).



Define the methods/techniques used to implement these tasks, including algorithms, models, or pipelines.



Document assumptions and limitations associated with tasks and methods.



Align tasks and capabilities with organizational goals, risk tolerance (MAP 1.5), and any regulatory constraints.



Record all information in a manner accessible to relevant AI actors for validation, oversight, and incident response.





Implementation Notes:



Provides the foundation for Map 2.2 (stakeholder and output documentation) and Map 2.3 (scientific integrity and TEVV).



Enables decision-making for deployment and human oversight configuration.





3\. GOVERN 1.1 – 1.7: Policies, Processes, Procedures, and Practices



GOVERN 1 Group Objective: Establish an organization-wide governance baseline for managing AI risks effectively, transparently, and consistently.



Subcategory - Requirement Summary



GOVERN 1.1



Understand, document, and actively manage legal and regulatory obligations related to AI systems.



GOVERN 1.2



Integrate characteristics of trustworthy AI (accuracy, reliability, explainability, fairness, privacy, and security) into organizational policies, processes, procedures, and practices.



GOVERN 1.3



Establish procedures to identify the appropriate level of risk management needed based on the organization’s risk tolerance.



GOVERN 1.4



Ensure transparency in risk management processes; document outcomes in line with organizational priorities and controls.



GOVERN 1.5



Implement ongoing monitoring and periodic reviews, clearly defining roles, responsibilities, and frequency for risk assessment updates.



GOVERN 1.6



Maintain an inventory of AI systems, including system attributes, responsible owners, and resources required in proportion to risk priority.



GOVERN 1.7



Define processes for safe decommissioning and phasing out of AI systems to prevent additional risks or loss of trustworthiness.



Implementation Notes:



These subcategories underpin accountability and transparency for AI operations.



Require documentation for internal audits and to satisfy regulators or ISO/IEC 42001 alignment efforts.



Should be coordinated with MAP tasks to align context, risk tolerance, and AI characteristics.



Summary Context



MAP 1.5 and MAP 2.1 link risk tolerance and system characterization to operational decision-making.



GOVERN 1.1–1.7 establish the foundational governance policies, review cycles, inventory, and risk documentation necessary to support responsible AI deployment.



Together, these requirements provide traceability from organizational risk appetite through system-specific characterization into regular governance and compliance checks.

Implementation guidance for MAP and GOVERN functions (EPIC)

**GDPR - Articles 5, 22, 35**



Articles 5, 22, and 35 of the GDPR set out fundamental principles of data processing, rules for automated decision-making, and mandatory data protection impact assessments to safeguard personal data.



Article 5 – Principles Relating to Personal Data Processing



Article 5 establishes the core data protection principles that all controllers and processors must follow when handling personal data



General Data Protection Regulation



These principles include:



Lawfulness, fairness, and transparency – personal data must be processed legally, fairly, and in a transparent manner.



Purpose limitation – data should only be collected for specified, explicit, and legitimate purposes and not further processed in incompatible ways.



Data minimization – only data that is adequate, relevant, and necessary for the purpose should be collected.



Accuracy – personal data must be accurate and kept up to date; inaccurate information must be rectified or erased.



Storage limitation – personal data should not be kept longer than necessary for the intended purposes.



Integrity and confidentiality – data must be processed securely to protect against unauthorized or unlawful processing, loss, or damage.



Accountability – the controller is responsible for demonstrating compliance with these principles. This principle requires maintaining records, audits, and documentation as proof of adherence





Article 22 – Automated Individual Decision-Making, including Profiling



Article 22 provides rules on decisions made solely by automated means that have legal or similarly significant effects on individuals.



Key points include:

Prohibition of solely automated decisions that affect individuals unless:

Explicit consent is given;

Such processing is necessary for a contract; or

Required by EU/Member State law.

Safeguards must exist to protect rights, freedoms, and legitimate interests, including the right to human intervention, to express one’s point of view, and to contest the decision.



This article primarily addresses profiling and algorithmic decision-making in contexts like credit scoring, recruitment, or insurance assessment.





Article 35 – Data Protection Impact Assessment (DPIA)



Article 35 mandates a Data Protection Impact Assessment (DPIA) when data processing is likely to result in high risks to individual rights and freedoms.



Essential elements include:

Assessment of risk – evaluating the potential impact and likelihood of privacy risks.

Measures and safeguards – identifying measures to mitigate identified risks.

Consultation – where high-risk processing remains even after mitigation, consultation with the data protection authority is required.

Documentation – the controller must document the assessment and keep it for accountability purposes.



Common scenarios requiring a DPIA include large-scale profiling, extensive processing of sensitive data, or systematic monitoring of public areas. Conducting a DPIA helps ensure compliance and demonstrates proactive data protection risk management.





Summary



Article 5: Establishes seven core principles guiding all personal data processing.



Article 22: Protects individuals from harmful decisions made solely by automated processing, requiring human oversight or legal justification.



Article 35: Requires a Data Protection Impact Assessment for high-risk processing to evaluate and mitigate risks to individual rights.



These articles collectively balance data utilization with fundamental rights, providing both a framework for lawful processing and protections against risk or discrimination in automated systems





**UK GDPR/DPA + FCA Consumer Duty**



The UK Financial Conduct Authority’s (FCA) Consumer Duty and the UK General Data Protection Regulation (UK GDPR) combined with the Data Protection Act 2018 (DPA 2018) interact primarily in areas where personal data processing underpins regulatory compliance and consumer protection. The equivalencies and intersections can be summarized as follows:



1\. Purpose Limitation and Lawful Basis for Data Processing



UK GDPR/DPA 2018:



Processing of personal data must have a lawful basis under Article 6 UK GDPR (e.g., consent, legitimate interests, legal obligation).



Special category personal data requires an additional lawful basis under Article 9, often balancing fairness, necessity, and proportionality.



FCA Consumer Duty:



Firms must gather and process customer information to deliver good outcomes for retail clients, assess product suitability, and measure outcomes across target markets.

Equivalence: data processing under Consumer Duty aligns with GDPR principles of purpose limitation—data collected for Consumer Duty purposes must be necessary, proportionate, and used only to meet regulatory obligations.





2\. Transparency and Customer Information



UK GDPR/DPA 2018:



Individuals must be informed about data processing in a concise, transparent, and intelligible manner (Articles 12–14).



This encompasses purpose, categories of data, retention, sharing, and rights.



FCA Consumer Duty:



Regulatory communications and engagement with customers to understand needs and outcomes must be clear and neutral, avoiding undue promotion.

Equivalence: Communicating Consumer Duty-driven processes with customers respects GDPR transparency principles, ensuring customers understand why their data is used to monitor outcomes and improve services.



3\. Data Minimization and Special Category Data



UK GDPR/DPA 2018:



Only data strictly necessary for a lawful purpose should be processed (Article 5), particularly careful with special category data (health, race, vulnerabilities).



FCA Consumer Duty:



Monitoring customer outcomes may require analyzing groups with protected or vulnerability characteristics.



Equivalence: the FCA notes firms should not systematically collect unnecessary special category data, but may use pre-existing information where lawful bases under Articles 6 and 9 exist—this aligns with GDPR data minimization obligations.





4\. Fairness and Avoiding Foreseeable Harm



UK GDPR/DPA 2018:



Processing must be fair and lawful, avoiding infringement of rights and interests of individuals (Article 5(1)(a)).

Impact on rights must be assessed, e.g., via Privacy Impact Assessments or DPIAs for high-risk processing.



FCA Consumer Duty:



Firms must act to avoid foreseeable harm, enable financial objectives, and ensure good outcomes for retail clients.



Equivalence: GDPR’s fairness principle parallels the Consumer Duty’s requirement to prevent consumer harm, including in algorithmic or AI-driven services.





5\. Governance and Accountability



UK GDPR/DPA 2018:



Controllers must demonstrate compliance through records of processing activities, Data Protection Impact Assessments (DPIAs), and appropriate safeguards.



FCA Consumer Duty:



Firms must develop strategies, governance frameworks, and monitoring protocols to assess outcome delivery.

Equivalence: documentation and monitoring under the Consumer Duty mirrors GDPR’s accountability principle, requiring firms to evidence that personal data processing supports good consumer outcomes.





6\. Direct Marketing and Communications



UK GDPR/DPA 2018 + PECR:



Direct marketing via electronic means requires consent or relevant exemptions, with opt-out rights.



FCA Consumer Duty:



Communications triggered by or for regulatory purposes (e.g., informing customers of better rates) do not automatically constitute direct marketing if neutral and factual.



Equivalence: respecting GDPR consent and opt-out rights ensures Consumer Duty communications are compliant with data protection regulations.





7\. Cookies and Tracking Technologies



UK GDPR/DPA 2018 + PECR:



Use of cookies for analytics or behavioral tracking generally requires prior opt-in consent, unless strictly necessary for delivering services.



FCA Consumer Duty:



Tracking customer engagement to meet Consumer Duty outcomes may invoke PECR rules.

Equivalence: such processing must adhere to GDPR principles of necessity and proportionality, obtaining consent where non-essential.





Summary Mapping Table

Conclusion



The UK GDPR/DPA 2018 and FCA Consumer Duty are highly complementary. Consumer Duty imposes obligations on firms that inherently require the processing of personal data in ways that must comply with GDPR principles: lawful, fair, transparent, proportionate, and accountable. Key equivalencies include data purpose limitation, fairness, transparency, governance, use of special category data, and consent requirements for communications or tracking. Proper alignment ensures both regulatory compliance and the delivery of good consumer outcomes.



For practical implementation, firms should document all Consumer Duty-related data processing, identify lawful bases (Articles 6/9), maintain DPIAs where appropriate, and ensure clear governance to satisfy both data protection law and FCA oversight simultaneously.







**DORA Articles 28 - 30**



Articles 28-30 of the Digital Operational Resilience Act (DORA) focus on the following key points:



Article 28 outlines the general principles for operational resilience, emphasizing the need for financial entities to maintain robust operational capabilities.



Article 29 mandates the proactive identification and ongoing assessment of third-party vulnerabilities, requiring financial entities to continuously monitor the security posture of third-party ICT providers.



Article 30 further elaborates on the requirements for incident reporting and the need for financial entities to have effective measures in place to manage operational disruptions.





For the full text and detailed provisions, you can refer to the official DORA regulation.



The Digital Operational Resilience Act (DORA, Regulation (EU) 2022/2554) establishes a comprehensive framework for the management of ICT third-party risks in the financial sector. Articles 28, 29, and 30 form the core of this framework, covering general principles, concentration risk assessment, and contractual requirements. Below is a detailed synthesis of their interrelations and practical implications:



Article 28 – General Principles and ICT Third-Party Strategy



Strategic Governance: Financial entities must integrate ICT third-party risk management into their overall ICT risk framework, maintaining board-approved strategies for outsourcing to ICT providers.



Proportional Risk Management: Risk management shall consider the nature, scale, and criticality of ICT dependencies, applying stricter controls for critical or important functions (CIFs).



Register of Information (RoI): Entities must maintain a central, up-to-date register documenting all ICT third-party arrangements, including provider identity, services, criticality, contract details, and data locations. This is both a management and supervisory tool, submitted to national competent authorities annually.



Pre-Contractual Due Diligence: Entities must assess provider suitability, information security, operational stability, regulatory compliance, and potential contribution to concentration risk.

Termination and Exit Strategies: Contracts must include enforceable termination rights. For critical functions, entities must define practical, tested exit strategies ensuring business continuity, regulatory compliance, and data portability.



Article 29 – ICT Concentration Risk Assessment



Identification of Concentration: Entities must identify risks stemming from dependency on a limited number of ICT providers (e.g., cloud services), including both single-provider and multi-function dependencies.



Risk Mitigation: Mitigation measures may include multi-vendor strategies, alternative solutions, and contractual provisions enabling transition if concentration risk threatens operational or systemic resilience.



Reporting: Concentration assessments, especially where critical services are at risk, must be documented and reviewed by the management body, reinforcing systemic monitoring across the sector.



Article 30 – Mandatory Contractual Provisions



Baseline Contractual Clauses (All ICT Services):



Clear service descriptions and data processing locations



Data protection, security measures, and availability commitments



Cooperation with competent authorities and incident notification



Termination rights and data recovery provisions



Enhanced Requirements for Critical or Important Functions:



Detailed quantitative and qualitative service-level targets



Unrestricted access, inspection, and audit rights



Data location and portability guarantees



Business continuity and sub-outsourcing controls



Participation in resilience testing and rapid incident resolution



Detailed exit and transition assistance obligations



Sub-outsourcing Controls: Providers must obtain prior consent for critical-function sub-outsourcing, ensure compliance of subcontractors with contractual obligations, and disclose all sub-contracting arrangements.





Interrelation in ICT Third-Party Risk Management



Article 28 lays the foundation by requiring strategic governance, proportional risk assessment, documentation via the RoI, and exit planning. It frames the lifecycle view of third-party risk.



Article 29 complements 28 by highlighting systemic and concentration risks, ensuring that reliance on a single or few providers is evaluated and mitigated.



Article 30 operationalizes 28 and 29 by mandating contractual clauses that enforce risk control, auditability, data protection, and continuity. Contracts become the legal instrument ensuring that strategic and concentration risks identified under Articles 28 and 29 can be practically managed and mitigated.



Together, these articles create a triad of governance, risk assessment, and enforceable contractual obligations, ensuring that financial entities maintain resilience even when critical operations are outsourced to third-party ICT providers.



Key Takeaways



DORA treats ICT third-party risk as systemic, not just operational. Risk cannot be outsourced.



The framework is tiered by service criticality, with more stringent requirements for CIFs.

Entities are expected to maintain accurate and auditable registers, perform pre- and post-contractual due diligence, and ensure exit capabilities.



These articles collectively enable supervisory oversight, accountability, and resilience, harmonizing EU practices beyond GDPR and traditional outsourcing guidance.



This summary integrates the procedural, contractual, and supervisory dimensions of ICT third-party risk management under DORA, emphasizing how Articles 28–30 interlock to secure operational and systemic resilience.







**ISO/IEC 27001:2022 Annex A 5.19 - 5.22**





Sections 5.19 to 5.22 of ISO 27001:2022 fall under Organizational Controls and specifically address information security within supplier and ICT supply chain relationships. These controls are designed to mitigate risks introduced through third parties and ensure that external partners comply with the organization’s security requirements.



The key points for each control are as follows:



5.19 – Information Security in Supplier Relationships (Annex A 15.1.1)



Objective: Extend the organization's information security policies and practices to encompass suppliers and external service providers.



Requirements:



Establish processes for identifying and managing risks related to suppliers’ products, services, and activities.

Ensure suppliers understand their security responsibilities and comply with the organization’s policies.

Mitigate the risks of unauthorized access, data breaches, or service disruption originating from supply chain partners.



5.20 – Addressing Information Security within Supplier Agreements (Annex A 15.1.2)



Objective: Incorporate enforceable security requirements into contractual arrangements with suppliers.



Requirements:



Define specific security obligations in supplier contracts based on the relationship type and associated risk.

Include clauses for data protection, incident reporting, audit rights, and compliance obligations.

Provide a legal framework to ensure suppliers adhere to required information security measures.



5.21 – Managing Information Security in the ICT Supply Chain (Annex A 15.1.3)



Objective: Manage risks linked to the supply of information and communication technology (ICT) components and services.



Requirements:



Implement processes to verify the security of ICT products and services obtained from suppliers.

Prevent the introduction of compromised or malicious hardware, software, or services into organizational infrastructure.

Monitor and manage end-to-end ICT supply chain risks to protect critical systems and sensitive information.



5.22 – Monitoring, Review and Change Management of Supplier Services (Annex A 15.2.1 \& 15.2.2)



Objective: Maintain ongoing oversight of supplier performance and ensure continuous compliance with agreed information security measures.



Requirements:



Regularly monitor and evaluate the supplier’s adherence to security obligations.



Conduct periodic audits or assessments to verify compliance with contractual and organizational security standards.



Manage changes in supplier services or procedures to prevent the introduction of new risks.

Ensure timely identification and remediation of deficiencies or security incidents affecting supplier-delivered services.



Context and Implementation Guidance



These controls ensure that supply chain and third-party risks are systematically addressed as part of the ISMS.



Implementation involves risk assessments, contractual clauses, monitoring frameworks, and continuous review mechanisms.



Organizations should document their approach and justify any exclusions in the Statement of Applicability (SoA).



Summary



Sections 5.19–5.22 emphasize risk management across suppliers and ICT supply chains, mandating a combination of contractual, procedural, and ongoing monitoring controls. They help organizations secure external dependencies, maintain compliance, and mitigate risks that could compromise confidentiality, integrity, and availability of information.



This ensures that the security posture of suppliers aligns with the organization’s ISMS objectives and regulatory obligations.
